**This Friday, send your CEO five lines.** Copy the template below, fill it in and send it before you log off. It takes about five minutes to write and two to read.

```
Wins:              what shipped or went well this week (one or two items)
Progress:          where the key milestone stands, on track or not
Blockers:          what is slowing us down, if anything
Decisions needed:  what I need from you, and by when
Next week:         what is coming
```

Do that every Friday for a month. The rest of this post explains why it works and what to add once it does.

## Why you're being micromanaged

Your team ships. Technical debt is under control. And your CEO still asks for "quick updates" that eat your afternoon.

The uncomfortable truth: **the problem isn't your delivery. It's your silence.**

Your CEO and board can't see engineering. They don't read pull requests or sit in retros. All they know is what you tell them. When you say nothing, they fill the gap with worry:

- **One quiet week:** "I wonder if something's wrong."
- **Two quiet weeks:** "Something is wrong."
- **A quiet month:** "We need someone to find out what's really going on."

You know exactly what's happening, so silence feels fine. They know nothing, so silence feels like a warning. "I'll flag issues when they come up" sounds, to them, like "I'll tell you when it's too late to do anything about it."

Regular updates are the price of autonomy.

## Build the rest in four weeks

Once the Friday email is a habit, add one piece a week. Each takes about 30 minutes.

**1. Week 1: list who needs to hear from you.** Start from this table and cross out anyone who doesn't apply:

| Who | What they need | How often | Format |
|-----|----------------|-----------|--------|
| CEO or founder | Progress, blockers, resource asks | Weekly | The Friday email |
| Board | Milestones, a few metrics, risks | Monthly | Dashboard plus a short narrative |
| Product | Timelines, technical constraints | Daily or weekly | Stand-up or Slack |
| Peer leads | Shared decisions, dependencies | Fortnightly | 1:1 or a shared doc |
| Finance | Budget, headcount, vendor costs | Monthly | A short note |

**2. Week 2: agree what "on track" means.** Book 30 minutes with your top three people from the table. Ask: "What does success look like for my team, in your eyes?" Leave with three to five things you'll track, and what red, amber and green look like for each.

**3. Week 3: lock the rhythm.** Put a recurring Friday-afternoon block in your calendar for the email. Same day, same format, every week. An irregular schedule tells people communication is an afterthought.

**4. Week 4: ask if it's useful.** Send one question: "Is this useful? What's missing?" Adjust, then write the system down so it survives your next holiday.

## The update nobody wants to send

The updates that matter most are the hard ones: we'll miss the date, the project is in trouble, I got something wrong.

My rule: **if something has worried me for more than 24 hours, I raise it.** Facts, context and a proposed next step. No drama.

Stakeholders cope with bad news. What they can't cope with is bad news that has been brewing for weeks. A delay raised in month one is a conversation. The same delay raised in month four is a crisis.

## What it looks like when it works

Consider a scenario where a CTO joins a Series B start-up. Her predecessor was let go after a platform migration ran six months late, and the board heard about it last. She sends a one-paragraph email every Friday and shows the same four metrics at every board meeting.

For two months the board chair reads every email and asks follow-up questions. By month three the replies get shorter. By month six the chair says: "You don't need to copy me on these any more. Just flag anything I should know about."

That's the goal. Not to report less, but to report so consistently that people stop worrying.

## Four ways this goes wrong

1. **Promising to end an awkward meeting.** A realistic "no" now beats a surprised "no" later.
2. **Sitting on problems.** Use the 24-hour rule.
3. **Skipping your technical peers.** They understand the technology, but they can't see your priorities.
4. **Letting the rhythm slip.** Missing one Friday is fine. Missing three in a row tells people updates aren't a priority, and the "quick updates" come back.

## Start now

Open a new email to your CEO, paste the five-line template and schedule it for Friday afternoon.
